# Marginalia and the Art of Active Reading

The practice of writing in book margins has a long and distinguished history. Darwin annotated his books heavily. Darwin's notes in the margins of works by Malthus show, in real time, the formation of natural selection. Jefferson's annotated books in the Library of Congress are a record of a mind in conversation with its sources.

We stopped teaching marginalia when we switched to digital reading, where annotation is optional and separate from the text. This was a mistake.

The act of marginal annotation changes reading from passive consumption to active dialogue. You are not absorbing content; you are arguing with it, extending it, noting its failures. The marginal note forces you to formulate a response — which requires understanding the claim being responded to.

**For digital reading:** Kindle highlights are not marginalia. They extract text without requiring you to formulate a response. Kindle notes — the annotations you actually type — are closer. But the friction of typing on a reading device reduces the frequency.

**For physical books:** Use pencil for uncertainty, pen for conviction. Write actual sentences, not just underlines. Date the annotations if you reread.

The practice connects to the Zettelkasten insight: you learn by processing, not by receiving. Marginalia is the simplest version of this principle.
