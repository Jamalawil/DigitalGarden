# The Wikilink as Thinking Tool

The `[[double bracket]]` syntax — the wikilink — is the most interesting piece of interface design in contemporary personal knowledge management.

Here is why: every wikilink is simultaneously a pointer to existing knowledge and a commitment to future knowledge. When I write `[[acoustic ecology]]` in a note, I am either linking to something I have already thought about or creating a placeholder for something I intend to think about. The note either exists or it doesn't. If it doesn't, it appears as a dangling link — a kind of intellectual IOU.

This is different from hyperlinking in a way that matters. When you insert a web hyperlink, you link to something that exists. Wikilinks can link to things that do not yet exist, and the very act of naming them is a kind of claim: this concept is real enough to deserve its own note.

## The Naming Problem

This creates an interesting problem: you have to name your concepts before you fully understand them. The choice of title for a note shapes what goes into it and how other notes relate to it.

I have several notes where I have used different names for the same concept across different documents. `[[News deserts]]` and `[[journalism voids]]` and `[[local news collapse]]` are three names I have used for approximately the same phenomenon. They are not linked to each other. This is a failure of my own naming discipline.

The solution — canonical note titles, redirects — is more infrastructure than I want to maintain for exploratory thinking. So I live with the inconsistency.

## Graphs Are Mostly Aesthetic

The graph view — the visualisation of notes as nodes connected by links — is the most-demoed feature of Obsidian and probably the least practically useful. Looking at a graph of your notes tells you almost nothing actionable. It is a map that does not help you navigate.

What the links actually do is: allow serendipitous retrieval. When I open a note on journalism and see links to notes on ecology, on knowledge management, on documentary film, I am reminded that these ideas are connected. This is useful. But it is useful because I am reading the note, not because I am looking at the graph.

## The Emergent Essay

The most interesting thing that has happened in my wikilinked notes is that some essays have assembled themselves. I have written a note, linked it to several other notes, returned to the first note and found that a coherent argument had accumulated in the margin. The links created a kind of scaffolding.

Whether this is what the PKM evangelists mean by emergent thinking, I am not sure. It might just be that writing things down helps you remember them.
