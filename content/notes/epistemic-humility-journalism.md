# Epistemic Humility in Fact-Checking

The fact-checking process in journalism is designed to catch errors. The standard is: can you support every material claim in your story with documentary evidence or on-the-record source confirmation?

This is a good standard. It catches a lot of errors. But it creates a subtle distortion: it conflates what you can *prove* with what you *know*.

Experienced journalists know many things they cannot prove. They know which officials routinely mislead the public. They know which companies have a history of regulatory evasion. They know the background that explains why a particular story is significant even when they cannot get that background on the record.

The published story contains only what can be proven. The reader gets a version of reality that is accurate but incomplete, and the incompleteness is structured — it systematically excludes the context that the reporter possesses but cannot document.

## The On-Record Problem

The reliance on on-the-record sources is a mechanism for producing checkable journalism. But sources who go on the record are not a random sample of people with knowledge. They are people with institutional standing, with public roles, with reasons to speak.

The people most likely to have direct knowledge of wrongdoing — employees, contractors, participants — are also the people with the most to lose from going on the record. Investigative journalism that relies only on named sources will systematically underrepresent the perspectives of the most directly affected.

## Living with Uncertainty

I am increasingly interested in journalism that marks its uncertainty explicitly — that shows the reader where the evidence is strong, where it is suggestive, and where the reporter is genuinely unsure. This is common in science journalism (where uncertainty is a core part of the story) and rare in accountability journalism (where confidence is treated as credibility).

The growth stage system in this garden is a small attempt at this. Noting whether an idea is a seedling, budding, or evergreen is a way of being honest about how developed and reliable it is.
