# On Being Embedded: Community Reporting in Practice

The conventional model of journalism imagines the reporter as an outsider who enters a story, gathers facts, and leaves to write. The distance is supposed to produce objectivity. The outsider sees what the insider cannot.

This model has real advantages. The fresh eye notices things the habituated eye has stopped seeing. The reporter who does not live in the community being covered has no personal stake in how powerful local figures are portrayed.

But the model also has real costs, which are less often discussed.

## What Distance Gets Wrong

The outsider-reporter misses context that is not explained because insiders assume everyone knows it. They miss the emotional register of events — what a particular development means to people, not just what it is. They are dependent on official sources and community leaders who have their own reasons to manage media coverage.

Perhaps most importantly, the outsider-reporter's presence is legible to the community as surveillance. People with reason to distrust outsiders — migrants, people navigating the informal economy, communities with fraught histories with media — perform for the reporter rather than speaking to them.

## What Embedded Reporting Offers

Embedded reporting — being a genuine member of the community you cover, not just a visitor — changes this dynamic. Trust accrues over time. People talk to you because they know you, not because you have shown up with a notebook.

The costs are real: you become a participant as well as an observer. The stories you choose to tell and not tell have consequences for relationships you value. The separation between reporter and subject, which journalism schools rightly teach as protective, dissolves.

I do not have a clean answer to these tensions. They are structural features of community journalism, not problems to be solved.

## Some Practical Observations

From working in this mode on various projects:

Time is the irreducible resource. Community trust cannot be shortcut. The reporters who do this work well have usually spent years, not months, in the communities they cover.

Transparency about your role matters more than confidentiality. People in tight communities usually know who you are writing for and why. Trying to obscure this is usually counterproductive.

The most important stories come from people who are not official sources. The community member who approaches you after a meeting, the person you have coffee with who mentions something in passing — this is where embedded reporting pays off.
