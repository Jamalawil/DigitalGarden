# The Iceberg Interview

Every source knows more than they tell you in the first pass. The visible part — the official account, the prepared response, the version they have told before — sits above the waterline. The journalist's job is to get below it.

The iceberg interview is a structure for doing this systematically.

## The Pattern

**Open wide, then narrow.** Begin with broad, open-ended questions that allow the source to establish their preferred framing. Do not challenge this framing yet. Listen for what they emphasise, what they skip over, and what they describe vaguely when you would expect specificity.

**Map the vagueness.** In the second phase, return to every vague moment in their account. "You said the process was complicated — can you walk me through exactly what happened?" This is where the interview begins to diverge from the source's prepared account.

**Follow the discomfort.** Sources become evasive around things that matter. Mild defensiveness — slight changes in tone, more carefully worded answers, sudden references to what they "can" and "can't" discuss — is a signal to stay. This is where you slow down, not speed up.

**Return to the same territory from different angles.** The story a source tells about a contested event will have gaps. Coming at the same period or decision from three different directions — chronologically, through the lens of other participants, through documentary evidence — surfaces contradictions that a single question would not.

**The final pass: the things they haven't mentioned.** Before ending, name the things that are conspicuously absent from the account. Not as accusations — as puzzles. "One thing I haven't heard you address is X. Help me understand where that fits."

## When It Works Best

Long-form investigative interviews where you have done significant pre-reporting. You need to know enough to recognise what is being omitted.

It is less suited to breaking news contexts where time does not allow the patient circling the pattern requires.

## The Ethical Dimension

The iceberg interview is a technique for extracting information from sources who may not fully intend to give it. This raises ethical questions that deserve acknowledgment.

The technique is justified when used in the service of accountability journalism — where the source has power and the information withheld affects the public interest. It is a different matter when used on vulnerable sources who are withholding information to protect themselves rather than to avoid accountability.

Journalists need to know which situation they are in.
