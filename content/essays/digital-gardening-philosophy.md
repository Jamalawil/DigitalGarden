# A Philosophy of Digital Gardening

Gardens are imperfect by design. Unlike a polished magazine article with a publication date and a fixed point of view, a digital garden is a living thing — it grows, gets pruned, and changes as your thinking evolves.

## What Makes a Garden Different

A blog post is a snapshot. A garden is a process.

When you publish a blog post, you implicitly claim: *this is what I think, fully formed, on this date*. A digital garden makes no such claim. Instead it says: *here is where my thinking is right now, and I'm still working on it.*

This distinction matters for two reasons:

1. It lowers the bar to publish
2. It changes the reader's relationship with the work

### Lowering the Bar to Publish

Most people have a graveyard of half-finished drafts that never see daylight. They're "not ready" — which usually means they're not perfectly polished. A digital garden inverts this: the rougher something is, the more clearly you signal it's in progress.

### Changing the Reader's Relationship

When you read a newspaper article, you read it as final. When you read a garden note, you read it as *in progress*. You expect it to be incomplete, speculative, even wrong in places. This is actually more honest — all ideas start somewhere rough.

## Growth Stages as Epistemic Markers

The growth stage system (🌱 Seedling, 🌿 Budding, 🌳 Evergreen) is an epistemic signal. It tells readers how much confidence to place in what they're reading.

- **Seedling**: Raw ideas, brainstorming, first drafts
- **Budding**: Taking shape, still evolving, probably right
- **Evergreen**: Stable, refined, will be updated but core argument is settled

## What This Garden Is For

This garden exists as a public record of my intellectual development. It's how I think out loud about journalism, knowledge management, ecology, and the intersections between them.

Some of this will be wrong. Some will be incomplete. All of it is honest.

Come back in six months. Things will have grown.
