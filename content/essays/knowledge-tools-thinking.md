# Do Knowledge Management Tools Change How We Think?

I have been using Obsidian seriously for two years and Roam Research before that for one year. In that time I have accumulated several thousand notes, a handful of evergreen essays, and a persistent uncertainty about whether the tools have made me think better or just think differently.

The question matters because the knowledge management community — Zettelkasten practitioners, digital gardeners, PKM evangelists — has a strong prior that the answer is yes. The tools, we are told, externalise cognition. They allow us to think thoughts we could not otherwise hold. They are, in the most ambitious version of the claim, cognitive prosthetics.

I am not sure this is true.

## The Strong Claim

The strong claim, made most explicitly by advocates of the Zettelkasten method, is that writing notes in a particular way — atomic, linked, in your own words — produces emergent insights that would not arise from reading alone.

The mechanism proposed is that linking notes forces you to identify conceptual connections, and that these connections, when accumulated, reveal patterns that your unaided mind would not perceive. The slip-box, as Niklas Luhmann described it, becomes a thinking partner.

There is something to this. I have had the experience of following a chain of links through my notes and arriving somewhere I did not expect. Connections surface that I did not consciously make.

## The Weak Counterargument

But the weak counterargument is also compelling: perhaps I would have arrived at those connections anyway, through the ordinary process of sustained reading and reflection. Perhaps the links I find are not emergent discoveries but retrievals of connections I had already made implicitly.

The problem is that I cannot run the counterfactual. I cannot know who I would be as a thinker without the tools, because I have had them for three years now and they have become part of my practice.

## What the Tools Do Well

Setting aside the strong claim, there are things knowledge management tools do well that are empirically verifiable.

**Retention**: I can find things. Not just the content of what I read, but my reaction to it, my questions about it, and how it connects to other things I have read. This is not a small thing. Most reading is forgotten almost entirely.

**Accumulation**: Arguments that require many pieces of evidence — the kind of arguments investigative journalism makes — can be built up gradually over time, with notes contributing as they accumulate. This is genuinely useful for long-form work.

**Honesty about not-knowing**: The practice of writing notes in your own words forces you to confront whether you actually understand something. It is easy to feel that you have understood a concept while reading; it is much harder to write that concept in your own words without revealing the gaps.

## The Harder Question

The harder question is whether all of this is what the tools' advocates promise: genuine cognitive augmentation, thinking we could not otherwise do. Or whether it is something more modest but still valuable: better organised retrieval and slightly less forgetting.

My current view is that the tools are more like very good filing systems than they are like cognitive prosthetics. They make the outputs of thinking more available to the thinker. They do not, by themselves, produce thoughts.

This may be enough. I am still using them. But I am using them with adjusted expectations.
