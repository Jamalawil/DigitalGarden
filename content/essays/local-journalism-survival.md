# How Local Journalism Survives the Attention Economy

Local journalism is dying. This is not a metaphor. Newsrooms have closed at a rate of roughly two per week in the United States for the past decade. The communities left behind — called "news deserts" — have measurably worse civic outcomes: lower voter turnout, higher municipal borrowing costs, less accountability for local institutions.

We understand the mechanism: digital advertising revenues, which once subsidised local news, migrated to platforms that aggregate attention at national and global scale. A local newspaper's readers are worth less to an advertiser than the same readers on Facebook, because Facebook can target them more precisely. The business model collapsed, and the journalism collapsed with it.

But something more interesting is happening now. A set of community-owned, reader-funded, and mission-driven newsrooms are demonstrating that local journalism can survive — if it is rebuilt on different foundations.

## Why the Old Model Failed

The legacy local newspaper was not, at its core, a journalism organisation. It was an advertising delivery vehicle that happened to produce journalism as a byproduct. The classifieds section, the car dealership ads, the supermarket circulars — these funded the city hall reporter and the crime beat and the school board coverage. When Craigslist and Google took the ads, the journalism went with them.

This matters because it means the crisis is structural, not editorial. Local newspapers did not die because they produced bad journalism. They died because the subsidy that made journalism economically viable was stripped away.

## The Community Models That Work

Three ownership structures are demonstrating resilience.

### Reader-funded newsrooms

Organisations like The Texas Tribune, The Marshall Project, and hundreds of smaller local outlets have rebuilt around direct reader support — subscriptions, memberships, and donations. The insight is that readers who benefit from journalism will pay for it if asked clearly and if the product is demonstrably valuable to them.

This model works best when the newsroom covers a clearly defined beat that readers care about enough to pay for. It is harder to sustain generalist newsrooms this way.

### Nonprofit and foundation-funded journalism

The shift to nonprofit status has allowed some newsrooms to pursue grant funding alongside reader revenue. This creates its own risks — dependency on foundations with their own agendas — but it has enabled some of the most ambitious local journalism of the past decade.

### Cooperative and community ownership

The most experimental model is direct community ownership — newsrooms structured as cooperatives or with community boards that formally represent reader interests. The Membership Puzzle Project has documented several of these. The governance structures are complex, but they create genuine alignment between the newsroom and the community it serves.

## What This Means for Coverage

These new models change the economics, but they also change the editorial logic.

A reader-funded newsroom is accountable to its readers in a way that an advertising-funded one is not. If your readers are predominantly small business owners, you cover small business. If they are parents of school-age children, you cover schools. This is not capture — it is alignment. The question is whether the community whose attention funds the newsroom is the same community that most needs the journalism.

In cities with high inequality, this alignment can be distorted: the wealthier residents who can afford to pay for journalism are not always the residents most affected by the institutions the journalism covers.

## The MARKA Thesis

My own project, MARKA, is an attempt to test these models in a specific context: underreported communities in which the gap between who pays for journalism and who needs journalism is large. The thesis is that community-embedded reporting — where journalists are genuinely part of the communities they cover — can close this gap.

This is a hypothesis. I am documenting it here as it develops.
