# Journalism in the Age of Loneliness

E.O. Wilson named it the Eremocene — the Age of Loneliness — to describe the geological era in which humanity has so thoroughly displaced the rest of the biosphere that other species live in perpetual isolation. Habitat fragments. Migration corridors collapse. Soundscapes hollow out. The sixth mass extinction proceeds not through one catastrophic asteroid but through a million small encroachments, each individually defensible, collectively annihilating.

What does this demand of journalism?

## The Problem of Scale

Extinction is not a story. Or rather, it is a story that resists every convention of narrative journalism. It has no single protagonist, no identifiable villain, no moment of crisis that doubles as a moment of decision. It unfolds across geological time and planetary space. It is, in the deepest sense, a systems story — and journalism was not built for systems.

We built journalism for events. Something happens; we report it. Someone says something; we quote them. But ecological collapse does not happen. It accumulates. And accumulation is almost impossible to narrate.

> The silence is data. The emptiness of a soundscape that was once dense with calls is as informative as any statistic — but you cannot quote a silence.

## Towards a New Ecology of Reporting

If we take the Eremocene seriously as a journalistic challenge, several things follow.

### Non-human subjects deserve narrative agency

The convention of quoting human sources — scientists, activists, officials — produces journalism that treats the living world as backdrop. The forests, reefs, and wetlands that are collapsing become settings for human drama rather than subjects of their own. This is not just an aesthetic failure. It is an epistemological one. It systematically underweights the interests and perspectives of the entities most affected.

This does not mean anthropomorphising animals or pretending that kelp forests have opinions. It means building reporting methodologies that take non-human life seriously on its own terms — that use acoustic ecology, population data, and species interaction networks to represent the interior life of an ecosystem, not just its surface.

### Journalists need longer time horizons

The news cycle is incompatible with ecological time. A story has to be new to be news — and ecological collapse is, by definition, not new. It is ancient and gradual and ongoing.

The digital garden model offers one response to this. Instead of producing stories with publication dates, we can produce working documents that are updated as our understanding grows. Notes that start as seedlings and are tended into evergreen pieces over years. This is not the same as a magazine's archive — it is a living record of understanding in progress.

### The duty to document what is being lost

There is a version of this argument that ends in paralysis: the scale is too large, the causes too diffuse, the solutions too uncertain. But the documentary impulse — the impulse to record — does not require certainty. It only requires presence.

The journalist's job in the Eremocene may be less to explain and more to witness. To be present in the places that are disappearing and to bring back records of what was there. This is not passive. Witness has always been a form of political act.

## What I Am Trying to Do

This garden is, among other things, an attempt to work out what Eremocene journalism looks like in practice. Some of what I am writing here is reportage — field notes, observations, documentation. Some of it is methodology — thinking through the tools and approaches needed to cover a story that resists coverage. Some of it is advocacy, which I try to mark clearly.

The Eremocene will not end because we wrote about it. But silence is the enemy. The choice to document what is being lost is itself a refusal of resignation.
